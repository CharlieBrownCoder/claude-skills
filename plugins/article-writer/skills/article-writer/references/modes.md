# The three modes

## playbook: a source document becomes an article

1. Read the source above any internal-only heading (safety pass, step 2).
   If the profile's source library classifies documents (a bucket, a status),
   confirm this one is cleared to become public content; if it is not, stop
   and say so.
2. Find the reader's question inside the document. A playbook is written for
   the person doing the work; the article is written for the person deciding
   whether, and how, to do it. Usually the article covers the why, the what
   and enough of the how to act, and the giveaway carries the steps.
3. Separate the method from its examples. The source's examples (a client, a
   keyword, a city) are not the reader's; replace them with examples the
   reader can map onto their own case, or anonymize them.
4. Keep the source's corrections and caveats; drop its hype. Where the
   source corrects a claim from its own origin, the article states the
   corrected version.
5. Then the brief, the safety pass, and the draft as usual.

## question: a customer question becomes an article

The owner's experience is the content; you collect it, then write it.

1. Settle the question. Use the customer's wording as the keyword when it is
   how people search; otherwise use the searched phrasing and keep theirs in
   the first H2.
2. Interview, one question at a time.
   - Ask up to 10 questions; offer a quick mode of 5 when the user is short
     on time.
   - Short, conversational questions: they may be answering by voice.
   - Acknowledge each answer in one sentence at most, then ask the next.
   - Do not summarize or analyze during the interview.
   - Skip a question the owner already answered; ask one follow-up ("Can you
     give me an example?") when an answer is thin, then move on.
3. The question bank (choose and adapt):
   - What does someone really mean when they ask this?
   - What is your background with it?
   - What is the biggest mistake people make?
   - What do most people think the answer is, and why is that wrong?
   - Walk me through a real case where this came up. What happened?
   - What did you try that did not work?
   - If a friend asked you this today, what would you tell them?
   - What is the one thing someone can do today?
   - What do you believe about this that most people in your field will not
     say?
   - What have we not covered?
4. Write only from the answers. Never invent a quote, a number, a result or a
   client. Anonymize any client the owner names unless they say the client
   agreed to be named. Use the owner's own phrasing wherever it passes the
   voice rules.
5. Byline the owner when the profile allows; the piece is theirs.
6. The report adds a line: the three to five things in the piece no generic
   article could include, each traced to an answer.

## refresh: an existing article is brought up to date

1. Keep the slug, the keyword and the original publish date. Set the
   updated date to the day the refresh goes live.
2. Re-run freshness on every claim in the article and every line of its
   source log. Update the log's date and lines.
3. Re-read the ranking pages for the keyword (method, step 3). Add what the
   field now covers that the article does not.
4. Re-check internal links: dead or moved pages, and newer pages that should
   now link here or be linked from here. With date causality, the article
   links only to pages dated before its original publish date, unless the
   profile says a refresh may link newer ones.
5. Run the checks. The report lists what changed and why, claim by claim.
